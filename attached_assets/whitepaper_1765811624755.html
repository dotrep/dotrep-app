.rep

.rep is a coordination primitive for permissionless systems. It derives non‑transferable trust signals from observable wallet behavior to support contextual access decisions under cheap identity.

Two wallets may perform the same actions, yet differ in the trust they earn over time.

Abstract

Permissionless systems cannot avoid access decisions. Any system that allocates scarce resources—attention, throughput, incentives, coordination—must determine eligibility in practice.

Under cheap identity, static credentials and cumulative scores collapse. Identities can be created faster than trust can be earned, and any fixed rule becomes an optimization target.

.rep reframes reputation as a property of behavior embedded in time and population structure. Trust is not accumulated or owned. It must be continuously maintained.

The Structural Constraint

Access‑control failures in open systems are not implementation bugs. They arise from a structural mismatch: evaluation is local and static, while incentives are global and adaptive.

If evaluation ignores either persistence over time or relational context, behavior becomes replayable. If both are ignored, reputation degenerates into accumulation.

Why These Dimensions Are Unavoidable

Any reputation system operating under cheap identity must answer two questions: whether behavior persists, and how it relates to the behavior of others.

Remove time, and the system cannot distinguish sustained participation from opportunistic execution. Remove relational position, and identical behavior becomes indistinguishable regardless of coordination.

Additional signals may refine evaluation, but they inevitably collapse into one of these two dimensions. Time and relational position are not design choices. They are the minimal degrees of freedom required to make synthetic participation expensive.

The Core Object

The behavior graph is the primary object of evaluation in .rep. All eligibility decisions are derived from its structure as it evolves over time.

Nodes represent wallet‑bound identities. Proximity reflects similarity of behavior within a shared context. Clusters, neighborhoods, and trajectories encode population structure directly.

Time as Maintenance Cost

Time in .rep is not a decay function applied to a score. It expresses the cost of maintaining coherence under observation.

Sustained participation produces smooth trajectories. Opportunistic strategies require frequent repositioning as incentives shift. Over time, these costs diverge.

Relational Position as Constraint

Relational position is not subjective context. It encodes how behavior sits relative to the population.

Coordinated or synthetic behavior tends toward dense, shallow clusters that must be continually adjusted. Organic participation produces broader, more stable neighborhoods.

Continuity

.rep evaluates behavior as a trajectory through relational space rather than as isolated events. Continuity is enforced as a constraint.

Natural participation deforms gradually. Synthetic strategies often require abrupt transitions that increase maintenance cost.

Identical Actions, Divergent Trust

Two wallets perform the same set of actions over the same period. Transactionally, they appear identical.

One integrates gradually into a stable neighborhood. Its trajectory is smooth and persistent. The other exhibits bursty participation aligned to incentives, repeatedly shifting relational position.

The difference is not behavior, but cost. One maintains coherence naturally. The other must continually re‑optimize to remain eligible.

Eligibility

.rep produces contextual eligibility signals, not universal scores. Eligibility is non‑transferable, continuously evaluated, and decays automatically.

Corollary

In permissionless systems, reputation cannot be accumulated without bound. It can only be maintained under constraint.

Cheap identity is not eliminated. Its dominance is reduced. Trust ceases to be owned and becomes an emergent property of sustained participation.
